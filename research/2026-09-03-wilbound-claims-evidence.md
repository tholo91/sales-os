# Executive finding

The PDF is the source of the claims, not evidence for them. None of the six claims is a universal scientific law. Two are supported well enough to become explicit Sales OS defaults: **test important assumptions before scaling commitment** and **pair clear goals with written progress monitoring**. Customer interviews and bounded autonomy are supported, but only with safeguards. “Always focus on one bottleneck” and “always automate before hiring” are useful operating heuristics, not proven general rules.

| Claim | Evidence verdict | Repository implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus on the current single biggest constraint | Conditional; strong operations logic, weak transfer evidence | Treat the constraint as a dated hypothesis and reassess it after each test |
| Interview customers and use their own language | Supported for discovering needs; exact-language and sales-impact claim is unproven | Preserve verbatim evidence, but separate quote, interpretation, and behavioral validation |
| Run low-cost experiments before scaling investment | Best-supported founder claim; some causal startup evidence | Require a hypothesis, predicted observation, cheapest discriminating test, and decision rule |
| Give clear outcome ownership and autonomy | Supported but mostly correlational and task-dependent | Assign one owner, outcome, boundaries, and feedback point; vary autonomy by task and capability |
| Simplify or automate repetitive work before hiring | Plausible heuristic; strict ordering is not proven | Use it as a capacity checklist, not a hiring gate |
| Combine goals with scorekeeping and feedback | Strong experimental/meta-analytic support, with feedback-design caveats | Record one outcome and one leading behavior; review task-focused evidence regularly |

# Claim-by-claim assessment

## 1. Focus on the current single biggest constraint

**What is supported.** In stable flow systems, throughput is limited by capacity constraints, so improving a non-constraint may not improve system throughput. A systematic review of 42 Theory of Constraints implementations in healthcare reported improvements in throughput and waiting time, but every included implementation was positive and much of the evidence consisted of case reports or conference proceedings. That makes publication bias and regression to the mean serious concerns ([Naor et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812771/)). A broader review likewise describes many applications but identifies gaps that have limited mainstream empirical acceptance ([Watson, Blackstone & Gardiner, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jom.2006.04.004)).

**Causal strength.** Strong mathematical/engineering rationale for stable sequential operations; weak causal evidence for a general management rule. The research does not establish that every knowledge-work or sales system has one independently identifiable constraint, nor that focusing exclusively on it always dominates a portfolio of learning activities.

**Founder-sales limit.** In early sales, the apparent bottleneck can move between targeting, trust, relevance, channel access, offer fit, founder time, and follow-up. Several may interact, and small samples make diagnosis noisy.

**Adaptation.** Keep one *current primary constraint* to reduce decision load, but label it as a dated hypothesis. Store: observed evidence, the metric it is expected to move, the smallest test, and the reassessment date. Do not block all other work on an asserted “single bottleneck.”

## 2. Direct customer interviews and customers' own language

**What is supported.** Direct interviews are an established method for identifying and structuring customer needs. Griffin and Hauser compared one-to-one interviews, focus groups, transcript coding, and need prioritization; importantly, they also showed that frequency of mention is not automatically importance and that common satisfaction measures can contain self-selection bias ([Griffin & Hauser, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.12.1.1)). A meta-analysis of customer participation in new-product development found positive performance relationships during ideation and launch, but customer participation during development could slow time to market and reduce financial performance ([Chang & Taylor, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.14.0057)).

A 23-month randomized field experiment with 274 small firms and 3,937 customers in Rwanda provides stronger causal evidence for *seeking and acting on private customer feedback*: treated firms increased revenue and profit relative to controls, made more feedback-resonant business changes, and low-intensity solicitation also worked, suggesting diminishing returns to asking more customers ([Kaul et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2023.0324)). It tested a formal feedback process among existing customers, not founder discovery interviews, exact wording, cold prospects, or European software/service sales.

Stated intent is also an imperfect proxy for action: a meta-analysis found that the intention–purchase relationship varies by whether the product is new, the time horizon, product type, question format, and outcome measure ([Morwitz, Steckel & Gupta, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijforecast.2007.05.015)).

**Causal strength.** Strong causal evidence for one small-business feedback intervention and moderate broader evidence that early customer participation improves need discovery and innovation outcomes. There is still weak direct evidence that copying customers' exact words causes better offers or sales. Verbatim language is high-value source data, not automatically a validated positioning statement.

**Adaptation.** Preserve short verbatim quotes and concrete past behavior in interaction records. Keep three fields distinct: `observed wording`, `founder interpretation`, and `behavioral evidence`. Prefer questions about the last real occurrence, current workaround, cost, and next action over hypothetical enthusiasm. Reuse customer phrasing in drafts only when it remains accurate and non-manipulative.

## 3. Run low-cost experiments before scaling investment

**What is supported.** This is the claim with the closest founder-specific causal evidence. In a randomized controlled trial of 116 Italian startups, both groups received market-feedback training, while the treatment group was additionally taught to state predictions and test hypotheses rigorously. Treated entrepreneurs performed better and pivoted more appropriately without becoming more likely to drop out ([Camuffo et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249)).

Separately, an observational study of high-technology startups found that adopting A/B testing was followed by 30%–100% performance improvements after a year and by more product launches and faster abandonment of weak ideas. Because adoption was not randomized, selection and concurrent organizational changes limit causal interpretation ([Koning, Hasan & Chatterji, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4209)).

**Causal strength.** Moderate causal support for disciplined hypothesis testing in startups; supportive observational evidence for a broader experimentation system. Neither study isolates “low cost” as the active ingredient or proves that every consequential decision can be reduced to an experiment.

**Adaptation.** Add a compact evidence contract before expensive work: assumption, prediction, cheapest safe discriminating test, success/failure threshold, maximum cost or time, and the decision each result triggers. A DM, one-question interview, manual demo, or payment/commitment ask should precede building when it can answer the same uncertainty.

## 4. Clear outcome ownership and autonomy

**What is supported.** A meta-analysis covering 72 sources, 83 samples, and 32,870 workers found that leader autonomy support correlated positively with autonomous motivation, well-being, and positive work behavior. It was primarily correlational and therefore does not establish causal performance gains ([Slemp et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30237648/)). A meta-analysis of 93 team studies found that autonomy and intrateam coordination were associated with better performance, but effects varied by task type ([Stewart, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206305277792)). Role ambiguity has a negative meta-analytic relationship with job performance, `r = -.21`, again with job-type moderators ([Tubre & Collins, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1177/014920630002600104)).

The important counterexample is task context: a quasi-natural retail field experiment found that less autonomy, through a specific instruction plus monitoring and feedback, increased sales and transactions in a narrow, routine job ([De Paola, Scoppa & Pupo, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2017.02.008)). The research does not isolate “one named outcome owner” as a causal treatment.

**Causal strength.** Moderate association-level support for clarity and autonomy; limited and mixed causal evidence. Autonomy is most defensible for complex work where local judgment matters, not as a blanket rule for every task.

**Adaptation.** For delegated Sales OS work, specify one owner, the outcome, decision boundaries, evidence standard, deadline, and review point. Give freedom over method inside those boundaries. Use tighter instructions for routine, high-risk, or legally constrained actions, and never interpret “ownership” as permission to send or publish externally.

## 5. Remove, simplify, or automate repetitive work before adding headcount

**What is supported.** Lean production research supports eliminating waste and using coherent process-improvement bundles. In an observational study of 1,757 manufacturing plants, lean bundles explained about 23% of variation in operational performance after controlling for industry and context ([Shah & Ward, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0272-6963(02)00108-0)). This does not establish a universal sequence of simplify, automate, then hire, and its manufacturing setting differs sharply from founder-led relationship work.

Automation can also relocate rather than eliminate work. The classic “ironies of automation” analysis explains how automated systems can leave humans with rarer, harder exception-handling tasks and weaker readiness to perform them ([Bainbridge, 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)). Firm-level observational evidence finds that automating firms may grow revenue and employment faster than nonautomating firms even while employment growth slows around the automation event, showing that automation and hiring are not simple substitutes ([Bessen et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/pandp.20201004)).

**Causal strength.** Weak for the claimed ordering. There is supportive evidence for waste reduction and context-dependent productivity benefits, but no good evidence here that adding headcount before automation is generally inferior.

**Adaptation.** Use a pre-hiring checklist: stop work with no validated value; simplify stable steps; automate only repetitive, observable, reversible work with defined failure handling; then compare the remaining capacity gap with the cost and learning value of hiring. Hire earlier when demand is persistent and the work depends on judgment, relationships, accountability, or exception handling.

## 6. Goal-setting plus scorekeeping and feedback

**What is supported.** Meta-analyses support specific, challenging goals over vague “do your best” goals, with effects moderated by task and setting ([Mento, Steel & Karren, 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(87)90045-8)). More directly, a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies with 19,951 participants found that interventions increasing progress monitoring improved goal attainment by `d = 0.40`; effects were larger when outcomes were physically recorded or reported publicly ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000025.pdf)).

Feedback is not automatically beneficial. A meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations found a positive average effect, `d = .41`, but more than one-third of feedback interventions reduced performance. Feedback was less effective when it shifted attention away from the task toward the self ([Kluger & DeNisi, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254)).

**Causal strength.** Strong experimental and meta-analytic support for explicit goals plus recorded progress monitoring. Feedback effects are heterogeneous, and narrow performance goals can be counterproductive for unfamiliar, complex work where learning goals are more appropriate.

**Adaptation.** Record one meaningful outcome metric and one leading behavior per active validation effort, with a short weekly evidence review. Keep feedback specific to the task, strategy, and next adjustment; avoid identity judgments and vanity dashboards. Use a learning goal such as “identify why five qualified people decline” when the correct strategy is not yet known, then switch to performance targets only after the process is understood.

# Recommended repository changes, in priority order

1. **Add an experiment contract** to validation workflows: hypothesis, prediction, cheapest discriminating test, threshold, budget, and next decision.
2. **Strengthen interaction evidence fields** so verbatim customer language, interpretation, stated intent, and observed behavior cannot be conflated.
3. **Add written progress monitoring** using one outcome and one leading indicator, reviewed with task-focused feedback.
4. **Frame constraint selection as revisable diagnosis**, not a universal “one bottleneck” fact.
5. **Use bounded autonomy for delegation**: owner, outcome, boundaries, evidence, deadline, review point.
6. **Keep simplify/automate-before-hire as a checklist**, not a mandatory gate or evidence-backed law.

# Bottom line

The scientifically defensible core is not “follow this operating doctrine.” It is: make assumptions explicit, collect direct but fallible customer evidence, run discriminating tests before large commitments, give people clarity plus bounded discretion, and record progress against meaningful goals. Sales OS should encode those behaviors while preserving the existing manual-action and evidence guardrails.
