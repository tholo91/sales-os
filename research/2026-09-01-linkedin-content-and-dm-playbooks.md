# Executive finding

Lara Acosta's SLAY framework is useful as a writing checklist for one post: story, lesson, actionable advice, and a handoff to the reader. Matt Lakajev adds the missing operating layer: content should point to a clear offer or learning goal, and public intent signals can start a relevant conversation. Neither framework proves that virality, follower growth, or the authors' self-reported revenue and meeting numbers will transfer to Brief nach Berlin.

# Lara Acosta

## Observed framework

Lara's public post describes four moves:

1. Start the first three lines with a story.
2. Put the lesson on line four.
3. Expand with actionable advice, usually a list.
4. End with a question that gives the reader the turn.

In a public video excerpt explaining the framework, she calls the fourth element “you” and frames the structure as a concise, actionable story and a “humble brag”. The useful principle is that the story earns attention and context before the lesson demonstrates expertise.

## What transfers

- Begin with a real scene, decision, failed attempt, or observed tension rather than a product announcement.
- State one narrow lesson, not a general claim about democracy or civic participation.
- Give the reader one or a few concrete actions they can try.
- Ask for a substantive experience or disagreement, not a generic “thoughts?”.

## What does not transfer automatically

- “Don't focus on facts” is too broad for a civic product. Facts, routing evidence, privacy boundaries, and source provenance are part of the trust proposition. Use emotion as the entry point, then keep the factual core visible.
- The suggested viral outcome and likes are distribution claims, not validation. A small number of replies from people with a recent issue is more useful for the current project.
- A manufactured personal story, inflated result, or “humble brag” would violate the project's evidence and voice rules.

# Matt Lakajev

## Repeated public claims and process

The accessible posts are authored by Matt Lakajev, who appears to be the person behind the supplied Matthew Lakajev YouTube handle. His posts repeatedly describe:

- Content aligned to one clear outcome or offer, rather than unrelated “personal brand” topics.
- A three-part system: content, conversations, and conversion optimisation.
- Two CTA patterns: comment for a resource, or click to access a resource.
- Intent-based outreach after a profile view, like, comment, connection, or CTA response.
- No immediate pitch. Ask questions, understand the situation, and only later ask whether help would be useful.
- LVQ in active DMs: acknowledge or mirror the person's point (“lube”), give personalized relevant value, then ask a question.
- A concrete next step after a positive answer; he often proposes times rather than sending a calendar link.
- Tracking activity and conversion separately: posts, DMs, connection requests, profile views, qualified leads, meetings, and deals.

Matt also publicly reports large meeting, revenue, follower, and client numbers. These are self-reported marketing claims; they are not treated as independent evidence here.

## What transfers to Brief nach Berlin

- Keep every post tied to the actual learning goal: can a person with a current public issue identify the responsible level, trust the routing, and take the next action?
- Use a real public signal to start a DM: a person's comment, question, or post about a concrete local/public problem. Do not mention hidden profile-view tracking as if it were personal knowledge.
- Replace the sales sequence with a validation sequence: acknowledge the issue, ask about the last real occurrence, offer a relevant observation or demo only when useful, then ask one easy feedback question.
- Measure relevant replies and completed issue-flow conversations, not only reach. The strongest current evidence would be someone verifying the recipient and stating what would make them handwrite and post the letter.
- Use one CTA per post. A resource CTA can be tested only if the resource exists, is genuinely useful, and can be delivered manually without pretending that a comment equals purchase intent.

## What does not transfer

- Do not copy high-volume connection-request, follow-up, or channel-switching cadences. They are designed for a high-ticket sales funnel and can feel invasive or spammy in a non-partisan civic context.
- Do not use “reciprocity” as a hidden obligation. Value should be offered because it is relevant, not to create debt.
- Do not pitch a paid offer or book a sales call: Brief nach Berlin is currently a free civic tool and the open question is trust and completion, not sales conversion.
- Do not infer intent from a like or profile view. Treat those as weak prioritization signals at most; public wording and direct answers remain the evidence.

## Additional transcript: “DM choreography”

The user-provided transcript adds one useful operational idea: after each reply, treat the conversation as a state transition rather than as an improvised stream. Check what the person said, what evidence is still missing, whether fit and readiness are present, and what single next action is justified. It also recommends making real work visible, using small natural message bubbles where that matches the sender's voice, and asking permission before sending an offer or calendar link.

The transcript also contains tactics that stay out of the shared framework: name-dropping for borrowed authority, manufactured or strategically amplified scarcity, high-volume follow-up, and treating a conversation as a predictable path to a booked call. These may fit Matt's high-ticket sales context but are not safe defaults for founder-led validation, civic work, or Thomas's voice.

# Recommended operating model

Use “SLAY + evidence + conversation”:

1. Story: one real moment from building, testing, or observing a civic letter flow.
2. Lesson: one narrow insight about the friction, routing uncertainty, or trust threshold.
3. Actionable: one to three concrete things a reader can check or try.
4. You: one question aimed at people who have actually faced the situation.
5. Evidence gate: verify every number, product state, routing claim, and quote.
6. Conversation loop: reply substantively, then invite one relevant person into a specific feedback exchange only when the public context supports it.

The first test should be one post about a real, already-observed friction point and one learning question. Success is a small number of detailed replies or a completed issue-flow conversation; likes and follower growth are secondary diagnostics.
