# Mindset for exposure, rejection, and asking for money

Use this before outreach sessions and calls, and whenever the founder describes avoidance, fear, or the urge to build instead of talk.

## Why the discomfort is the signal

- Paul Graham's [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html) names the failure mode directly: founders avoid recruiting users by hand out of a mix of shyness and laziness, because approaching real people risks rejection. The manual, awkward path is the one that works at the start.
- Reframe: an outreach session's output is learning, not approval. A "no" that explains itself is a successful outcome and belongs in the evidence register.
- If the founder has spent more sessions improving the product or the system than talking to people, name it. Building is the socially safe form of procrastination for technical founders.

## Rejection math

- Silence and rejection are the base rate, not a verdict on the founder or the product. Expect most first touches to produce nothing; plan volume of honest attempts, not perfection of one message.
- Track conversations started per week, never messages polished. Two sound drafts are enough; the third revision is avoidance (see `conversation-principles.md`).
- One concrete cure for rejection fear is exposure in small doses: one uncomfortable ask per day beats a planned campaign that never ships.

## Compliments are the trap

- Rob Fitzpatrick's [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/) working rule: people will lie kindly. Compliments, "sounds cool", and vague future promises are noise. Only past behaviour, introductions, time commitments, and money are signal.
- Therefore the scary question ("would you pay for this?", "can we book the pilot?") is also the only question that produces real evidence. Avoiding it does not protect the relationship; it only delays the truth.

## Asking for money

- Charging is a validation instrument, not an imposition. Early adopters buy benefits and outcomes; they are far less price-sensitive than founders fear ([YC, Startup Pricing 101](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)).
- Asking for payment is how you find out whether the problem is real. A free pilot postpones the riskiest assumption; a paid or payment-adjacent commitment tests it.
- Never apologize for a price. State it, tie it to the outcome, and let the person react. Their reaction is data either way (see `strategies/pricing.md`).

## Pre-call state

- Nervousness before a call is normal and does not need to be fixed; it needs a job. Convert it into curiosity about the person's last real occurrence of the problem.
- The founder is not performing; the other person is the interesting one. A validation call where the founder talks less than a third of the time is usually a good call.
- Lower the stakes honestly: this is one conversation of many, and its worst realistic outcome is a logged "no-fit" that sharpens the ICP.

## Two-minute pre-outreach reset

Before opening LinkedIn, email, or a community thread:

1. Read the person's actual context once.
2. Say the message aloud as if you were continuing a relevant conversation with a capable peer, not trying to win a stranger.
3. Check that there is one honest reason to write and one small question they can decline.
4. Stop editing after two sound drafts. Send manually when it is truthful and specific, then record the attempt.

## What this file must never justify

Motivation talk is not a substitute for action. If this file is being read instead of sending an approved draft, the next step is sending the draft.
